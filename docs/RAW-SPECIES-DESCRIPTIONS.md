## Amaranthus tuberculatus

https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/75400-Amaranthus-tuberculatus

A distinguishing characteristic of tall waterhemp that sets it apart from similar members of the genus _[Amaranthus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amaranthus "Amaranthus")_ is the lack of hair on its [stems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_stem "Plant stem") and [leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf "Leaf"). This characteristic gives the leaves a bright, glossy appearance. The stem is typically erect and slender, which can be up to three feet long with a color of either green or red.[[8]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/75400-Amaranthus-tuberculatus#cite_note-Gower-8)

Tall waterhemp is a [dioecious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dioecious "Dioecious") plant. The seedhead branches in the female are numerous, short, and smooth. The male seedhead branches are fewer, longer, and more slender than those of the female.

The species has terminal spike [inflorescences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflorescence "Inflorescence") and very short [bracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bract "Bract") with simple to highly branched flowers. Seed produced is reddish to black and less than 1/32 inch in diameter.[[10]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/75400-Amaranthus-tuberculatus#cite_note-Stritzke-10)

Tall waterhemp is a [summer annual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summer_annual "Summer annual") that produces a large number[_[quantify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Dates and numbers")_] of very small[_[quantify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Dates and numbers")_] seeds. It is considered an [r-strategist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R-strategist "R-strategist"). [Emergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergence "Emergence") can span several months and often occurs later in the season than other annual weeds, allowing the weed to evade typical weed control strategies such as [herbicide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbicide "Herbicide") application and [tillage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tillage "Tillage").[[8]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/75400-Amaranthus-tuberculatus#cite_note-Gower-8) One study observed 80% emergence not occurring until ten weeks after the initial emergence.[[13]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/75400-Amaranthus-tuberculatus#cite_note-13) Extreme temperatures have little effect on seed viability. [Germination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germination "Germination") occurs typically after soil temperature alternation, as this is required to break seed dormancy.[[14]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/75400-Amaranthus-tuberculatus#cite_note-14) Waterhemp has been found to germinate in a wide range of soil and temperature conditions.[[15]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/75400-Amaranthus-tuberculatus#cite_note-isu-15) It has been found to germinate 17 years after seed set.[[16]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/75400-Amaranthus-tuberculatus#cite_note-16)

While tall waterhemp cannot [self-pollinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-pollination "Self-pollination"), due to having separate male and female plants, it does not require any vectors for pollination.[[15]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/75400-Amaranthus-tuberculatus#cite_note-isu-15) This allows for wind pollination over large distances, generating a large amount of [genetic diversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_diversity "Genetic diversity"). Another factor contributing to [genetic diversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_diversity "Genetic diversity") is the large amount of seed produced. Tall waterhemp in competition with [soybean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soybean "Soybean") has been reported to produce from between 300,000 and 5,000,000 seeds per plant.[[17]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/75400-Amaranthus-tuberculatus#cite_note-j-17) Tall waterhemp also has a rapid growth rate, 50–70% greater than other annual weeds.[[18]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/75400-Amaranthus-tuberculatus#cite_note-18)

## Ambrosia artemisiifolia
https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/53587-Ambrosia-artemisiifolia

_Ambrosia artemisiifolia_ is an [annual plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annual_plant "Annual plant") that emerges in late spring. It propagates by seed.[[4]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/53587-Ambrosia-artemisiifolia#cite_note-Native_Plant_Information_Network-4)

It is much-branched, and grows up to 70 centimetres (2.3 ft) in height.[[9]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/53587-Ambrosia-artemisiifolia#cite_note-Jepson_eFlora-9) The pinnately divided soft and hairy leaves are 3–12 centimetres (1.2–4.7 in) long.[[9]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/53587-Ambrosia-artemisiifolia#cite_note-Jepson_eFlora-9)

Its bloom period is July to October in North America.[[9]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/53587-Ambrosia-artemisiifolia#cite_note-Jepson_eFlora-9) Its pollen is wind-dispersed, and can be a strong [allergen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allergen "Allergen") to people with [hay fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allergic_rhinitis "Allergic rhinitis").[[4]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/53587-Ambrosia-artemisiifolia#cite_note-Native_Plant_Information_Network-4)[[10]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/53587-Ambrosia-artemisiifolia#cite_note-10)

It produces 2–4 mm [obconic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obconic "Obconic") green to brown fruit.[[9]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/53587-Ambrosia-artemisiifolia#cite_note-Jepson_eFlora-9) It sets seed in later summer or autumn. Since the seeds persist into winter and are numerous and rich in oil, they are relished by [songbirds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songbird "Songbird") and [upland game birds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upland_game_bird "Upland game bird").[[4]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/53587-Ambrosia-artemisiifolia#cite_note-Native_Plant_Information_Network-4)

The species name, _artemisiifolia_, is given because the leaves were thought to bear a resemblance to the leaves of [Artemisia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemisia_\(genus\) "Artemisia (genus)"), the true [wormwoods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemisia_absinthium "Artemisia absinthium").

## Bassia scoparia
https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/159194-Bassia-scoparia

_**Bassia scoparia**_ is a large annual herb in the family [Amaranthaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amaranthaceae "Amaranthaceae") (_[sensu lato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensu_lato "Sensu lato")_) native to Eurasia. It has been [introduced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introduced_species "Introduced species") to many parts of [North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_America "North America"),[[1]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/159194-Bassia-scoparia#cite_note-plants-1) where it is found in grassland, prairie, and desert shrub ecosystems.[[2]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/159194-Bassia-scoparia#cite_note-feis-2) Its [common names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_name "Common name") include **summer cypress**,[[2]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/159194-Bassia-scoparia#cite_note-feis-2) **mock-cypress**, **kochia**, **belvedere**, **World's Fair plant**, **burningbush**,[[1]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/159194-Bassia-scoparia#cite_note-plants-1) **Mexican firebrush**, and **Mexican fireweed**,[[3]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/159194-Bassia-scoparia#cite_note-GRIN-3) with the latter three names referring to the herb's red autumn foliage.

The fruit of _Bassia scoparia_ with the [calyx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calyx_\(botany\) "Calyx (botany)") attached is dull brown, but when hulled, it reveals dull black seeds,[[4]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/159194-Bassia-scoparia#cite_note-egginton-4) or dark to blackish-brown seeds in some escaped regions, such as Europe.[[5]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/159194-Bassia-scoparia#cite_note-5)

The seeds are dispersed by wind and water and are transported when the whole plant detaches and rolls on the wind as a [tumbleweed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumbleweed "Tumbleweed").[[2]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/159194-Bassia-scoparia#cite_note-feis-2) The seed does not persist in the [soil seed bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_seed_bank "Soil seed bank"), dying within about a year if it fails to germinate.[[2]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/159194-Bassia-scoparia#cite_note-feis-2) The species is a [C4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C4_carbon_fixation "C4 carbon fixation") plant, specifically of the NADP-ME type.[[6]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/159194-Bassia-scoparia#cite_note-kadereit-6)[[7]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/159194-Bassia-scoparia#cite_note-7) It develops [herbicide resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbicide_resistance "Herbicide resistance") unusually quickly,[[8]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/159194-Bassia-scoparia#cite_note-Kumar-et-al-2018-8) and quadruple-resistant populations have developed in [North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_America "North America").[[8]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/159194-Bassia-scoparia#cite_note-Kumar-et-al-2018-8)

_Bassia scoparia_ is native to a region ranging from Central Europe to Asia and is now widespread throughout the world. While having a variety of beneficial uses, it is also considered an invasive weed.[[1]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/159194-Bassia-scoparia#cite_note-plants-1)

## Chenopodium album
https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58127-Chenopodium-album

_**Chenopodium album**_ is a fast-growing [annual plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annual_plant "Annual plant") in the [flowering plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowering_plant "Flowering plant") family [Amaranthaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amaranthaceae "Amaranthaceae"). Though cultivated in some regions, the plant is elsewhere considered a [weed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weed "Weed"). Common names include **lamb's quarters**, **melde**, **goosefoot**, **wild spinach** and **fat-hen**, though several are also applied to other species of the genus _[Chenopodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chenopodium "Chenopodium")_, for which reason it is often distinguished as **white goosefoot**.

It tends to grow upright at first, reaching heights of 10–150 centimetres (4–59 in), rarely to 3 m; it then typically becomes recumbent after flowering (due to the weight of the foliage and seeds) unless supported by other plants. The [leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaves "Leaves") are alternate and varied in appearance. The first leaves, near the base of the plant, are toothed and roughly diamond-shaped, 3–7 cm long and 3–6 cm broad. The leaves on the upper part of the flowering stems are entire and lanceolate-rhomboid, 1–5 cm long and 0.4–2 cm broad; they are waxy-coated, unwettable and mealy in appearance, with a whitish coat on the underside. The small [flowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower "Flower") are radially symmetrical and grow in small cymes on a dense branched [inflorescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflorescence "Inflorescence") 10–40 cm long.[[4]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58127-Chenopodium-album#cite_note-pier-4)[[5]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58127-Chenopodium-album#cite_note-fna-5)[[6]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58127-Chenopodium-album#cite_note-foc-6) Further, the flowers are bisexual and female, with five tepals which are mealy on outer surface, and shortly united at the base.[[7]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58127-Chenopodium-album#cite_note-vicflora-7) There are five stamens.[[7]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58127-Chenopodium-album#cite_note-vicflora-7)


## Cockspur grass
https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60283-Echinochloa-crus-galli

_**Echinochloa crus-galli**_ is a species of wild grass originating from tropical Asia that was formerly classified as a [panicum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panicum "Panicum") grass. It is commonly known as **cockspur**, **cockspur grass**, **barnyard grass**, **barnyard millet**, **water grass**, **cocksfoot grass**, **cockshin grass**.[[4]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60283-Echinochloa-crus-galli#cite_note-GRIN-4) It is sometimes referred to using the general term, _barnyard grass_, which may refer to any species of the genus _[Echinochloa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echinochloa "Echinochloa")_. This plant can grow to 1.5 m (5 ft) in height and has long, flat leaves which are often purplish at the base. Most stems are upright, but some will spread out over the ground. Stems are flattened at the base. The seed heads are a distinctive feature, often purplish, with large millet-like seeds in crowded [spikelets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spikelet "Spikelet").

Considered one of the world's worst weeds, it reduces [crop yields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_yields "Crop yields") and causes [forage crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forage_crops "Forage crops") to fail by removing up to 80% of the available [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil "Soil") [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen "Nitrogen"). It acts as a host for several [mosaic virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosaic_virus "Mosaic virus") diseases.[[5]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60283-Echinochloa-crus-galli#cite_note-fao-5) Heavy infestations can interfere with mechanical harvesting.

Individual plants can produce up to 40,000 seeds per year. Water, birds, insects, machinery, and animal feet disperse it, but contaminated seed is probably the most common dispersal method.

## Common corn cockle
https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/75310-Agrostemma-githago

_**Agrostemma githago**_, the **common corn-cockle** (also written "corncockle"), is a herbaceous annual [flowering plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowering_plant "Flowering plant") a member of [Caryophyllaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caryophyllaceae "Caryophyllaceae"), also called the pink family or the carnation family of plants. The name of this genus is derived from Greek: _agros_ (ἀγρός) “field” and _stemma_ (στέμμα) “garland, crown."[[1]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/75310-Agrostemma-githago#cite_note-:0-1)

It grows with a stem to 100 cm (39 in) long with lanceolate leaves. The flowers are up to 5 cm (2.0 in) in diameter, usually single at the ends of the stem. The sepals have five narrow teeth much longer than the petals. It has ten stamens.[[2]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/75310-Agrostemma-githago#cite_note-2) It has slender pink flowers. It is an erect plant covered with fine hairs. Its few branches are each tipped with a single deep pink to purple flower. The flowers are scentless, 25–50 mm (1.0–2.0 in) across, and produced in the summer months – May to September in the northern hemisphere, November to March in the southern hemisphere.

Each petal bears two or three discontinuous black lines. The five narrow pointed sepals exceed the petals and are joined at the base to form a rigid tube with ten ribs. Leaves are pale green, opposite, narrowly lanceolate, held nearly erect against stem and are 45–145 mm (1.8–5.7 in) long. Seeds are produced in a many-seeded capsule. It can be found in fields, roadsides, railway lines, waste places, and other disturbed areas.[[1]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/75310-Agrostemma-githago#cite_note-:0-1)

## Cornflower
https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/933404-Centaurea-cyanus

_**Centaurea cyanus**_, commonly known as **cornflower** or **bachelor's button** (among other names), is an annual [flowering plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowering_plant "Flowering plant") in the family [Asteraceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteraceae "Asteraceae") native to Europe. In the past, it often grew as a [weed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weed "Weed") in cornfields (in the broad sense of "corn", referring to [grains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_grain "Food grain"), such as wheat, barley, rye, or oats), hence its name.

_C. cyanus_ is now [endangered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endangered_species "Endangered species") in its native habitat by [agricultural intensification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture "Agriculture"), particularly by over-use of [herbicides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbicide "Herbicide"). However, it is now also [naturalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalisation_\(biology\) "Naturalisation (biology)") in many other parts of the world, including North America and parts of Australia.

_Centaurea cyanus_ is an [annual plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annual_plant "Annual plant") growing to 20–100 centimetres (8–39 in) tall, with grey-green branched stems. The leaves are lanceolate and 3–10 cm (1–4 in) long.[[1]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/933404-Centaurea-cyanus#cite_note-FNA-1) The flowers are most commonly an intense blue colour and arranged in [flowerheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflorescence "Inflorescence") (capitula) of 1.5–3 cm diameter, with a ring of a few large, spreading ray [florets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floret "Floret") surrounding a central cluster of disc florets. The blue pigment is [protocyanin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protocyanin "Protocyanin"), which in roses is red.[[2]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/933404-Centaurea-cyanus#cite_note-2) Fruits are approx. 3.5 mm long with 2–3 mm-long [pappus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pappus_\(botany\) "Pappus (botany)") bristles.[[3]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/933404-Centaurea-cyanus#cite_note-3) It flowers all summer.[[4]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/933404-Centaurea-cyanus#cite_note-The_Wild_Flower_Key-4)

## Creeping thistle
https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense

_**Cirsium arvense**_ is a perennial species of [flowering plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowering_plant "Flowering plant") in the family [Asteraceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteraceae "Asteraceae"), native throughout [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe") and western [Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia "Asia"), northern Africa and widely [introduced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introduced_species "Introduced species") elsewhere.[[2]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-2)[[3]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-jncc-3)[[4]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-4)[[5]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-5) The standard English name in its native area is **creeping thistle**.[[6]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-bsbi-6) It is also commonly misnomered as **Canada thistle** and sometimes as **field thistle**.[[7]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-7)[[8]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-8)

_Cirsium arvense_ is a [C3 carbon fixation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C3_carbon_fixation "C3 carbon fixation") plant.[[11]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-Sigurd-11) The C3 plants originated during [Mesozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesozoic "Mesozoic") and [Paleozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleozoic "Paleozoic") eras, and tend to thrive in areas where sunlight intensity is moderate, temperatures are moderate, and ground water is plentiful. C3 plants lose 97% of the water taken up through their roots to transpiration.[[12]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-Raven2001-12)

Creeping thistle is a [herbaceous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbaceous "Herbaceous") [perennial plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perennial_plant "Perennial plant") growing up to 150 cm, forming extensive [clonal colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clonal_colony "Clonal colony") from thickened roots that send up numerous erect shoots during the growing season.[[13]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-:0-13) It is a [ruderal species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruderal_species "Ruderal species").[[14]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-14)

Given its adaptive nature, _Cirsium arvense_ is one of the worst invasive weeds worldwide. Through comparison of its genetic expressions, the plant evolves differently with respect to where it has established itself. Differences can be seen in their R-protein mediated defenses, sensitivities to abiotic stresses, and developmental timing.[[15]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-15)

Stems are 30–150 cm, slender green, and freely branched,[[20]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-:1-20) smooth and [glabrous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glabrousness#In_botany "Glabrousness") (having no [trichomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichomes "Trichomes") or [glaucousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaucousness "Glaucousness")), mostly without spiny wings. Leaves are [alternate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phyllotaxis "Phyllotaxis") on the stem with their base sessile and clasping or shortly [decurrent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decurrent "Decurrent"). The [leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf "Leaf") are very spiny, lobed, and up to 15–20 cm long and 2–3 cm broad (smaller on the upper part of the flower stem).

The [inflorescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflorescence "Inflorescence") compound cyme is 10–22 mm (0.39–0.87 in) in diameter, pink-purple, with all the florets of similar form (no division into disc and ray florets). The flowers are usually [dioecious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dioecious "Dioecious"), but not invariably so, with some plants bearing hermaphrodite flowers.[[20]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-:1-20) The [seeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seed "Seed") are 4–5 mm long, with a feathery [pappus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pappus_\(flower_structure\) "Pappus (flower structure)") which assists in wind dispersal.[[26]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-fnwe-26)[[27]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-blamey-27)[[28]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-kay-28) One to 5 flower heads occur per branch, with plants in very favourable conditions producing up to 100 heads per shoot.[[13]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-:0-13) Each head contains an average of 100 florets. Average seed production per plant has been estimated at 1530. More seeds are produced when male and female plants are closer together, as flowers are primarily insect-pollinated.[[13]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-:0-13) The plant can bloom from seed in a year then subsequently the seeds produced can emerge in the following year.[[29]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/60132-Cirsium-arvense#cite_note-rapidcityjournal.com-29)
## Eclipta

https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/76765-Eclipta-prostrata
_**Eclipta prostrata**_, the **false daisy** or **tattoo plant**, is a species of plant in the family [Asteraceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteraceae "Asteraceae").[[3]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/76765-Eclipta-prostrata#cite_note-GRIN-3)[[4]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/76765-Eclipta-prostrata#cite_note-usda-4) It is widespread across much of the world.[[5]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/76765-Eclipta-prostrata#cite_note-5)[[6]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/76765-Eclipta-prostrata#cite_note-6)[[7]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/76765-Eclipta-prostrata#cite_note-7)

This species is native to North and South America. It also grows commonly in moist places in warm temperate to tropical areas worldwide. It can be found in habitats such as on marsh edges, along lake shores and river banks, and in brackish marshes and cypress depression swamps. It is considered to be a weed.[[8]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/76765-Eclipta-prostrata#cite_note-Weeds_of_the_South-8)[[9]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/76765-Eclipta-prostrata#cite_note-9)

This plant has fibrous cylindrical, grayish roots from a shallow taproot. Solid, circular, purplish stems with white fine hairs, and grows between 0.3 and 1.5 m in height. [Leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf "Leaf") arranged in opposite pairs, hairy in two-sided, lanceolate, serrated 2–12.5 cm long, 5–35 mm wide. The solitary perfect [flower heads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower_heads "Flower heads") are 6–8 mm (0.24–0.31 in) in diameter, with white florets. The bumpy achenes are compressed and narrowly winged with the papus attached at the apex, as well as an inconspicuous tuft of small trichomes.[[8]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/76765-Eclipta-prostrata#cite_note-Weeds_of_the_South-8)[[10]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/76765-Eclipta-prostrata#cite_note-steenis1981-10)

## Eleusine indica
https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/76790-Eleusine-indica

_**Eleusine indica**_, the **Indian goosegrass**,[[1]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/76790-Eleusine-indica#cite_note-1) **yard-grass**,[[2]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/76790-Eleusine-indica#cite_note-BSBI07-2) **goosegrass**, **wiregrass**, or **crowfootgrass**, is a species of grass in the family [Poaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poaceae "Poaceae"). It is a small annual grass distributed throughout the warmer areas of the world to about 50 degrees latitude. It is an [invasive species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasive_species "Invasive species") in some areas.

_Eleusine indica_ is closely related to _[Eleusine coracana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleusine_coracana "Eleusine coracana")_ (finger millet or African finger millet), and the diploid _E. indica_ is likely an ancestor of the allotetraploid _E. coracana_.

Seeds of _E. indica_ are edible and are sometimes used as a [famine food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Famine_food "Famine food"), but yields are low. It is an important weed of cultivated crops, lawns, and golf courses. It thrives in disturbed areas with compacted soils in full sun. Both tillage and herbicides are used in its control. This low-growing grass is capable of setting seed even when closely mown. Some populations have evolved resistance to certain [herbicides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbicide "Herbicide"), including [glyphosate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glyphosate "Glyphosate").

_Eleusine indica_ performs C4 photosynthesis and therefore can grow in hot climates and in the hotter months of the temperate zone. Its seeds germinate later in spring than most other temperate zone weeds, such as [crabgrasses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digitaria "Digitaria"). Though usually considered an annual, it may survive for more than a year in climates not subject to frost.

## Euphorbia maculata

https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58893-Euphorbia-maculata

_**Euphorbia maculata**_, known as **spotted spurge**, **prostrate spurge** (not to be confused with _[Euphorbia prostrata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphorbia_prostrata "Euphorbia prostrata")_), **milk purslane**, or **spotted sandmat**, is a fast-growing [annual plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annual_plant "Annual plant") in the family [Euphorbiaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphorbiaceae "Euphorbiaceae"). It is native to North America, where it is generally considered a common weed. It can be found in disturbed soils such as garden beds, along railroad tracks, and in the cracks of sidewalks.[[3]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58893-Euphorbia-maculata#cite_note-3) It has become a common [introduced species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introduced_species "Introduced species") throughout the world, including Europe, Japan, Korea, Australia, and New Zealand.[[4]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58893-Euphorbia-maculata#cite_note-4)

It grows in sunny locations and a variety of soils, and functions as a [pioneer species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pioneer_species "Pioneer species") in [ecological succession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_succession "Ecological succession"). The sap of this plant is a mild skin irritant and can cause a rash in some people.[[5]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58893-Euphorbia-maculata#cite_note-5) The milky sap of plants in genus _Euphorbia_ is poisonous and considered carcinogenic.[[6]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58893-Euphorbia-maculata#cite_note-6)

As originally described in 1753 by [Carl Linnaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Linnaeus "Carl Linnaeus"), _Euphorbia maculata_ is an herbaceous plant with small, oblong opposite leaves. The leaves are pilose, or covered in small thin hairs, and generally have a [crenate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crenation "Crenation") (scalloped) edge. Each leaf is marked with a small brown spot in the center, giving the plant its common name.[[7]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58893-Euphorbia-maculata#cite_note-7)

_Euphorbia maculata_ is typically prostrate, with specimens rarely reaching as high as 30 centimetres (12 in). The stems spread out in a mat along the ground with each stem rarely greater than 45 centimetres (18 in) long. The [leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf "Leaf") are oval but rather elongate, up to 3 centimetres (1.2 in) long. The [cyathia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyathium "Cyathium"), bisexual reproductive structures unique to plants in the genus, are very small, with four white petal-like appendages that are sometimes pink.[[8]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58893-Euphorbia-maculata#cite_note-8)

_Euphorbia maculata_ flowers in the summer. These flowers develop in clusters near the axillary bud, they are small and leaf-like, with a red calyx. Upon blooming they have lobed, white to pink petals.[[9]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58893-Euphorbia-maculata#cite_note-:0-9)

_Euphorbia maculata_ develops a small, 3-lobed fruit. This fruit, like the rest of the plant, is covered in fine, soft hairs. Each lobe is a capsule that contains a single seed. The seeds are small, oblong, and white to light brown. The surface is uneven and covered in little divots.[[9]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58893-Euphorbia-maculata#cite_note-:0-9)

_Euphorbia maculata_ is similar to _[Euphorbia prostrata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphorbia_prostrata "Euphorbia prostrata")_, but that species has shorter leaves that are more rounded at the tips. It may occasionally be confused with _[Euphorbia serpens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphorbia_serpens "Euphorbia serpens")_ but this taxon has very short and rounded leaves and larger (but still inconspicuous) cyathia.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

## Field bindweed

https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/53021-Convolvulus-arvensis

_**Convolvulus arvensis**_, or **field bindweed**, is a species of [bindweed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convolvulus "Convolvulus") in the [Convolvulaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convolvulaceae "Convolvulaceae")[[1]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/53021-Convolvulus-arvensis#cite_note-1) native to [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe") and [Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia "Asia"). It is a [rhizomatous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhizomatous "Rhizomatous") and climbing or creeping [herbaceous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbaceous "Herbaceous") [perennial plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perennial_plant "Perennial plant") with stems growing to 0.5–2 metres (1.6–6.6 ft) in length. It is usually found at ground level with small white and pink flowers.

Other [common names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_name "Common name"), mostly obsolete, include **lesser bindweed**, **European bindweed**, **withy wind** (in [basket willow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basket_willow "Basket willow") crops), **perennial morning glory**, **small-flowered morning glory**, **creeping Jenny**, and **possession vine**.

_Convolvulus arvensis_ is a [perennial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perennial_plant "Perennial plant") vine that typically climbs to 1 metre (3.3 ft). The vine produces woody [rhizomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhizome "Rhizome")[[5]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/53021-Convolvulus-arvensis#cite_note-FOC-5) from which it resprouts in the spring, or when the aboveground vines are removed.

The [leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf "Leaf") are spirally arranged, linear to arrowhead-shaped, 2–5 cm (0.79–1.97 in) long and alternate, with a 1–3 cm (0.39–1.18 in) petiole. The [flowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower "Flower") are trumpet-shaped, 1–2.5 cm (0.39–0.98 in) diameter, white or pale pink, with five slightly darker pink radial stripes. Flowering occurs in mid summer[[6]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/53021-Convolvulus-arvensis#cite_note-Clapham1968-6) (in the UK, between June and September[[7]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/53021-Convolvulus-arvensis#cite_note-Readers-7)) when white to pale pink, funnel-shaped flowers develop. Flowers are approximately 1.9–2.5 cm (0.75–0.98 in) across and are subtended by small bracts. The fruit are light brown, rounded and 3–4 mm (0.12–0.16 in) wide. Each fruit contains 2 or 4 seeds that are eaten by birds and can remain viable in the soil for decades. The stems climb by twisting around other plant stems in a counter-clockwise direction.[[6]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/53021-Convolvulus-arvensis#cite_note-Clapham1968-6)

## Field milk thistle
https://weeds.org.au/profiles/perennial-sow-thistle/

Perennial Sow-thistle (_Sonchus arvensis_) is a perennial (long lived) herb with long creeping roots (rhizomes) or creeping stems (stolons), spreading vegetatively, with other plants shooting and growing from the creeping roots and stems. The entire plant is filled with a milky white sap. The stems are upright, simple or branched (mainly near the top), finely grooved or ribbed, hollow and 50-150 cm high, with two to many stems per plant. The leaves are alternate, variable in size, and variably divided, with coarsely toothed edges and pointed lobes which are bent downward. They are firm and dark green, paler on the lower surface, hairless and lance-shaped. The rosette and lower stem leaves are 15-35 cm long and 3-10 cm wide, divided about halfway to the midrib. The upper leaves are less lobed, and are narrow-triangular to awl shaped. The stem leaves clasp the stem and do not have stalks (Cooke 1986; Garnock-Jones 1988; Stace 1997).

The **inflorescence** (flower structure) is branched, and consists of numerous **flower-**heads. The flower stalks are 1-5 cm long, hairless or densely glandular hairy. Each flower-head is surrounded by a graded series of 25-45 bracts (modified leaves), which are hairless or are covered with tangled short, white branching hairs, and long yellow glandular hairs. The flower head consists entirely of ligulate florets (petal-like flowers), and has both male and female parts. There are no tubular florets. The flower heads are 40-50 mm across. The blades of the ligulate florets are yellow, spreading, and up to 3 cm long (Cooke 1986; Garnock-Jones 1988; Stace 1997). Each flower produces a seed with many seeds produced on a flower-head.

The **fruits** or 'seeds' (cypselas) are dark brown, not winged, and are narrowly oval in shape, weakly flattened, 2.5-3.8 mm long, and 1.0-1.5 mm wide, with 4-6 lengthwise ribs on each face. The ribs and the spaces between them are conspicuously transversely wrinkled. The top of the fruit has a tuft (pappus) of numerous silky, white, parachute-like hairs, 10-14 mm long, that are not shed (Cooke 1986; Garnock-Jones 1988; Stace 1997).

For further information and assistance with identification of Perennial Sow-thistle, contact the herbarium in your state or territory.

## Geraniums

https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/47699-Geranium-maculatum

Geranium maculatum, the wild geranium, spotted geranium, or wood geranium, is a perennial plant native to woodlands in eastern North America, from southern Manitoba and southwestern Quebec south to Alabama and Georgia and west to Oklahoma and South Dakota.[2][3]

It is a perennial herbaceous plant growing to 60 cm (2 ft) tall, producing upright, usually unbranched stems and flowers in spring to early summer. The leaves are palmately lobed with five or seven deeply cut lobes, 10–12.5 cm (4–5 in) broad, with a petiole up to 30 cm (12 in) long arising from the rootstock. They are deeply parted into three or five divisions, each of which is again cleft and toothed.

The flowers are 2.5–4 cm (1.0–1.6 in) in diameter, with five rose-purple, pale or violet-purple (rarely white) petals and ten stamens. In the Northern Hemisphere, they appear from April to June (precise dates depend on the latitude).[3][4] They are grouped in loose corymbs or umbels of two to five at the top of the flower stems.[5]

The fruit capsule, which springs open when ripe, consists of five cells each containing one seed joined to a long beak-like column 2–3 cm (0.8–1.2 in) long (resembling a crane's bill) produced from the center of the old flower.[3]

The rhizome is long, and 5 to 10 cm (2 to 4 in) thick, with numerous branches. It is covered with scars, showing the remains of stems of previous years' growth. When dry it has a somewhat purplish color internally.[6]

## Hybrid goosefoot
https://www.minnesotawildflowers.info/flower/maple-leaf-goosefoot

Numerous tiny flowers are tightly packed in rounded clusters (glomerules) in spike-like and branching arrangements at the top of the stem, at the tips of branching stems, and arising from upper leaf axils. Within a glomerule, flowers may be at different stages of development, some just budding and others with maturing fruit.

Flowers lack petals, have 5 stamens and a round, green ovary with a 2-parted style at the tip. Cupping the flower is a green calyx with usually 5 lobes up to 1 mm long, occasionally 4 lobes. Flowers are sometimes pinkish and may turn red with maturity. Bracts are absent. The calyx and stalks are smooth to sparsely white-mealy.
  
Leaves are alternate, 1½ to 6+ inches long, up to about 4 inches wide, triangular to egg-shaped in outline, irregularly lobed around the edge, the lobes like large, sharply pointed teeth with a broad, rounded sinus between, the base heart-shaped to straight across. Lower leaves are largest on stalks up to about 1 inch long, upper leaves usually smaller and shorter-stalked. Surfaces are bright green, hairless and smooth, sometimes sparsely white-mealy when young but becoming smooth. Stems are usually erect, unbranched to much branched, green and smooth except in the flower clusters.

Fruit is a dry seed enclosed in the persistent ovary shell (pericarp) that matures from green to brown and may loosely wrap the seed or is tightly adhered to it. Fruit in the glomerule is all arranged horizontally.

Seeds are flattened round to egg-shaped, 1 to 2 mm long, shiny black, faintly pitted or wrinkled to nearly smooth on the surface.

Maple-leaf Goosefoot, known in some references as _Chenopodiastrum simplex_, _Chenopodium gigantospermum_ or _Chenopodium hybridum_, is an annual found mostly in moist, usually shaded, rocky or sandy soils and is less weedy than most other [_Chenopodium_](https://www.minnesotawildflowers.info/search?kw=Chenopodium) species. It is distinguished from the others primarily by the leaves, which are larger than average with a few large, sharply pointed teeth-like lobes and a broad, rounded sinus between teeth. Leaves are not white-mealy, or only sparsely so when young. The flower or fruit clusters are also smooth to sparsely white-mealy, the calyx usually has 5 lobes, the clusters are more heavily branched than most others in the genus, and seeds are shiny black and larger than average, 1 to 2 mm long.

It is one of the easier _Chenopodium_ species to identify. By comparison, other species may have leaves that have rounded lobes or not lobed at all, are consistently white-mealy on one or both leaf surfaces, are densely white-mealy in the flower and fruit clusters, have only 3(4) calyx lobes, and/or seeds less than 1 mm long, have a more deeply pitted or wrinkly texture on the surface, and/or are brown.

## Ipomoea indica
https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/77496-Ipomoea-indica

_**Ipomoea indica**_[[3]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/77496-Ipomoea-indica#cite_note-3) is a [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species "Species") of [flowering plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowering_plant "Flowering plant") in the [family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_\(taxonomy\) "Family (taxonomy)") [Convolvulaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convolvulaceae "Convolvulaceae"), known by several common names, including **blue morning glory**, **oceanblue morning glory**, _**koali awa**_, and **blue dawn flower**. It bears heart-shaped or three-lobed leaves and purple or blue funnel-shaped flowers 6–8 cm (2–3 in) in diameter, from [spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_\(season\) "Spring (season)") to [autumn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autumn "Autumn"). The flowers produced by the plant are [hermaphroditic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermaphrodite "Hermaphrodite"). This plant has gained the [Royal Horticultural Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Horticultural_Society "Royal Horticultural Society")'s [Award of Garden Merit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Award_of_Garden_Merit "Award of Garden Merit").[[4]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/77496-Ipomoea-indica#cite_note-4)[[5]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/77496-Ipomoea-indica#cite_note-5)

_Ipomoea indica_ is a vigorous, long-lived, [tender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardiness_\(plants\) "Hardiness (plants)"), [perennial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perennial_plant "Perennial plant") plant, a [vine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vine "Vine") which is [native](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_plant "Native plant") to tropical, subtropical and warm temperate habitats throughout the world. It blooms all year long[[1]](https://books.google.com/books?id=XLFQEAAAQBAJ&dq=%22tecoma+stans%22%2B%22honey%22&pg=SA2-PA29). They can most commonly be found in disturbed forests, forest edges, secondary woodland, suburban gullies, and along roadsides and waterways. The plant climbs well over other plants, walls and slopes as growing on the bottom. Its climbing habit allows it to compete with trees and shrubs successfully. It is a twisting, occasionally lying, [herbaceous plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbaceous_plant "Herbaceous plant") which is more or less densely hairy on the axial parts with backward-looking [trichomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichome "Trichome"). The stems can grow 3 to 6 centimetres (1.2 to 2.4 in) long and sometimes have roots at the nodes.

The leaves are petiolate with 2 to 18 centimetres (0.79 to 7.09 in) long [petioles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petiole_\(botany\) "Petiole (botany)"). The leaf blade is egg-shaped or round, 5 to 15 centimetres (2.0 to 5.9 in) long and 3.5 to 14 centimetres (1.4 to 5.5 in) wide. The underside is densely hairy with short, soft trichomes, the top is more or less sparsely hairy. The base is heart-shaped, the leaf margin is entire or three-lobed, the tip is pointed or sharply pointed.

The [crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_\(botany\) "Crown (botany)") is funnel-shaped, 5 to 8 centimetres (2.0 to 3.1 in) long, [glabrous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glabrousness_\(botany\) "Glabrousness (botany)"), bright blue or bluish purple, with age they become reddish purple or red. The centre of the crown is a little paler.

_I. indica_ is a long-lived plant that can live up to 25 years.[[7]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/77496-Ipomoea-indica#cite_note-:0-7)
## Meadow grass

https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/52809-Poa

_**Poa**_[[2]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/52809-Poa#cite_note-2) is a [genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genus "Genus") of about 570 [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species "Species") of [grasses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poaceae "Poaceae"), native to the temperate regions of both hemispheres. Common names include **meadow-grass** (mainly in Europe and Asia), **bluegrass** (mainly in North America), **tussock** (some [New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand "New Zealand") species), and **speargrass**. _Poa_ ([πόα](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CF%80%CF%8C%CE%B1#Ancient_Greek "wikt:πόα")) is [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") for '[fodder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fodder "Fodder")'. _Poa_ are members of the subfamily [Pooideae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pooideae "Pooideae") of the family [Poaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poaceae "Poaceae").[[3]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/52809-Poa#cite_note-3)[[4]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/52809-Poa#cite_note-4)[[5]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/52809-Poa#cite_note-5)[[6]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/52809-Poa#cite_note-6)[[7]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/52809-Poa#cite_note-30001404-2-7)

Bluegrass, which has green leaves, derives its name from the seed heads, which are blue when the plant is allowed to grow to its natural height of two to three feet (0.6 to 0.9 meters).[[8]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/52809-Poa#cite_note-8)[[9]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/52809-Poa#cite_note-9)[[10]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/52809-Poa#cite_note-10)

The genus _Poa_ includes both [annual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annual_plant "Annual plant") and [perennial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perennial_plant "Perennial plant") species. Most are [monoecious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_sexuality "Plant sexuality"), but a few are [dioecious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_sexuality "Plant sexuality") (separate male and female plants). The [leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf "Leaf") are narrow, folded or flat, sometimes bristled, and with the basal sheath flattened or sometimes thickened, with a blunt or hooded apex and membranaceous [ligule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligule "Ligule").[[11]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/52809-Poa#cite_note-11)
  
They feature **narrow, linear leaf blades** that often end in a boat-shaped tip, with sheaths that are open or closed and membranous **ligules**.

_**Mercurialis perennis**_, commonly known as **dog's mercury**, is a poisonous woodland plant found in much of [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe") as well as in Algeria, Iran, Turkey, and the Caucasus, but almost absent from [Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland "Ireland"), [Orkney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orkney "Orkney") and [Shetland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shetland "Shetland").[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercurialis_perennis#cite_note-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercurialis_perennis#cite_note-2) A member of the spurge family ([Euphorbiaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphorbiaceae "Euphorbiaceae")), it is a [herbaceous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbaceous_plant "Herbaceous plant"), downy perennial with erect stems bearing simple, serrate leaves. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercurialis_annua
_**Mercurialis annua**_, **annual mercury**, is a species of [flowering plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowering_plant "Flowering plant") in the spurge family [Euphorbiaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphorbiaceae "Euphorbiaceae"). It is native to the Middle East and the Mediterranean region, where it grows on bare, sandy soils. Its seeds are dispersed by harvester ants, which remove an oily coating that delays germination. In recent centuries, annual mercury has spread to northern Europe and many other parts of the world as an agricultural and urban weed. It has been studied for its complex genetics and breeding system. The name is from the Roman god Mercury, due to its association with fertility.

Annual mercury is an [annual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annual_plant "Annual plant") [herb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbaceous_plant "Herbaceous plant") which typically grows to about 30–50 cm (exceptionally, up to 1 m) tall,[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercurialis_annua#cite_note-Lockton-1) with an erect, branched stem that has two strong ridges along its length, changing sides at each node. The whole plant is more-or-less [glabrous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glabrousness "Glabrousness") and, when broken, it exudes a watery sap (not a white latex, as other Euphorbiaceae do). It is easily uprooted to reveal a bundle of thin, fibrous roots.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercurialis_annua#cite_note-Stace-2)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercurialis_annua#cite_note-Sell3-3)

The leaves are arranged in opposite pairs and have a short petiole about 1 cm long (exceptionally up to 4 cm), with tiny green stipules, 1–2 mm long, at the base. Each leaf is about 7 cm long by 3.5 cm wide, ovate with a pointed tip, and up to about 10 (range: 4–18) blunt teeth along each side, each with a [hydathode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydathode "Hydathode") at the tip.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercurialis_annua#cite_note-Sell3-3)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercurialis_annua#cite_note-Poland-4)

Plants can be male, female or [hermaphrodite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermaphrodite "Hermaphrodite"), giving rise to [dioecious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dioecy "Dioecy"), [monoecious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoecy "Monoecy"), [gynodioecious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gynodioecy "Gynodioecy") or [androdioecious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Androdioecy "Androdioecy") populations, which is very unusual in the [plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant "Plant") kingdom. Male plants have their flowers arranged at the tips of long stalks ([peduncles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peduncle_\(botany\) "Peduncle (botany)")) arising from the leaf axils and projecting beyond the leaves. Female plants have their flowers in sessile clusters of 1–4 at the nodes of the branches, or on short (4 mm) pedicels. Hermaphrodite flowers, where these occur, have a similar arrangement to the female flowers. Sometimes these are functionally female, producing no pollen or even lacking [stamens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stamen "Stamen") entirely.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercurialis_annua#cite_note-Pannell2004-5)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercurialis_annua#cite_note-Pannell2008-6)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercurialis_annua#cite_note-7)

Even in the north of its range, annual mercury can usually be found flowering all year round.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercurialis_annua#cite_note-Crawley-8) The flowers of both sexes have 3 [perianth segments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perianth "Perianth") which look like small yellowish-green petals about 2 mm long. The males have 8–12 stamens which are somewhat longer than the tepals, with yellow anthers that turn black after the pollen is released. Female flowers have two ovaries and two [styles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stigma_\(botany\) "Stigma (botany)"), with bristle-tipped [tubercles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tubercle "Tubercle").[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercurialis_annua#cite_note-Sell3-3)

The fruit is a capsule, or [schizocarp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schizocarp "Schizocarp") up to about 4 mm long with a warty, spiky coat, which splits into two mericarps as it matures. Each mericarp contains one shiny brown seed which is about 2 mm in diameter and has a [caruncle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elaiosome "Elaiosome") at one end. These are explosively expelled from the mericarp as it dries and turns inside-out, being fired about 30 cm (small seeds might go as far as 130 cm) from the parent plant.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercurialis_annua#cite_note-Lisci1997-9)

## Taraxacum officinale
https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/47602-Taraxacum-officinale

_**Taraxacum officinale**_, the **dandelion** or **common dandelion**,[[6]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/47602-Taraxacum-officinale#cite_note-6) is a herbaceous [perennial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perennial "Perennial") flowering plant in the daisy family, [Asteraceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteraceae "Asteraceae"). The common dandelion is well-known for its yellow [flower heads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower_head "Flower head") that turn into round balls of many [silver-tufted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pappus_\(botany\) "Pappus (botany)") fruit, which [disperse in the wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_dispersal "Wind dispersal"). These balls are sometimes called "clocks" or "blowballs".

Originally native to [Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasia "Eurasia"), as a result of its hardiness and easy propagation, the dandelion has become widely established across several continents. It has been introduced to southern Africa, the Americas, Australia, and New Zealand. It grows in [temperate regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate_regions "Temperate regions") of the world in areas with moist soils. They are able to grow in a variety of environments and are tolerant of crowding, extreme temperatures, and low moisture.

The dandelion is often considered a [weed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weed "Weed"), especially in lawns, but it is increasingly being recognised in its native regions as useful for attracting birds and pollinating insects. In one study, it ranked as the fourth most important source of pollen. Although dandelion pollen is considered to be an early food resource for some emerging pollinators in spring, it has also been found to lack certain essential nutrients and can lead to declining brood health for bees.[[7]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/47602-Taraxacum-officinale#cite_note-7) The leaves, flowers, and roots are sometimes used as food and in [herbal medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_herb "Medical herb").

_Taraxacum officinale_ grows from a (generally unbranched) [taproot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taproot "Taproot") and produces several hollow, leafless flower stems,[[8]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/47602-Taraxacum-officinale#cite_note-Rose-2006-8): 470  typically 5–40 centimetres (2–15+3⁄4 inches) tall, but occasionally reaching up to 70 cm (28 in) tall. The stems can be tinted purplish, are upright or lax, and produce [flower heads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower_head "Flower head") that are held as tall or taller than the foliage. The foliage may be upright-growing or horizontally spreading; the leaves have petioles that are either unwinged or narrowly winged. The stems can be [glabrous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glabrousness_\(botany\) "Glabrousness (botany)") or sparsely covered with short hairs. Plants have milky [latex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latex "Latex"), and the leaves are all [basal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basal_leaf "Basal leaf"); each flowering stem lacks [bracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bract "Bract") and has one single flower head. The yellow flower heads lack receptacle bracts, and all the flowers, which are called [florets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floret "Floret"), are ligulate and bisexual. In many lineages, the seeds are mostly produced by [apomixis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apomixis "Apomixis"),[[9]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/47602-Taraxacum-officinale#cite_note-Morley-1969-9) even though the flowers are frequently visited by diverse insects.[[10]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/47602-Taraxacum-officinale#cite_note-10)

The leaves are 5–45 cm (2–17+3⁄4 in) long and 1–10 cm (1⁄2–4 in) wide, and they are oblanceolate, oblong, or obovate in shape, with the bases gradually narrowing to the petiole. The leaf margins are typically shallowly lobed to deeply lobed and often lacerate or toothed with sharp or dull teeth.[[9]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/47602-Taraxacum-officinale#cite_note-Morley-1969-9)

The calyculi (the cuplike bracts that hold the florets) are composed of 12 to 18 segments; each segment is reflexed and sometimes glaucous. The lanceolate-shaped bractlets are arranged in two series, with acuminate apices. The 14–25 millimetres (1⁄2–1 in) wide involucres are green to dark green or brownish-green, with dark grey or purplish tips. The florets number 40 to over 100 per head, having yellow or orange-yellow corollas.

The fruit, called [cypselae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cypselae "Cypselae"),[[11]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/47602-Taraxacum-officinale#cite_note-New_York_Botanical_Garden-11) range in colour from olive-green or olive-brown to straw-coloured or greyish; they are oblanceoloid in shape and 2–3 mm (1⁄16–1⁄8 in) long, with slender beaks. The fruit have 4 to 12 ribs that have sharp edges. The silky [pappi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pappus_\(botany\) "Pappus (botany)"), which form the parachutes, are white to silver-white in colour and around 6 mm wide. Plants typically have 24 or 40 pairs of chromosomes, while some have 16 or 32 pairs.[[12]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/47602-Taraxacum-officinale#cite_note-Efloras-2-12)

## Thorn apple

https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium

_**Datura stramonium**_, known by the common names **thornapple**, **jimsonweed** (**jimson weed**) or **stink blade**, is a poisonous [flowering plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowering_plant "Flowering plant") in the _[Daturae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Datureae "Datureae")_ [tribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribe_\(botany\) "Tribe (botany)") of the nightshade family [Solanaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solanaceae "Solanaceae").[[1]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-POWO-1) Its likely origin was in [Central America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_America "Central America"),[[1]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-POWO-1)[[2]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-2) and it has been introduced in many world regions.[[3]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-NPGS/GRIN-3)[[4]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-4)[[5]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-5) It is an aggressive [invasive weed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasive_species "Invasive species") in [temperate climates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate_climate "Temperate climate") and [tropical climates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_climate "Tropical climate") across the world.[[1]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-POWO-1) _D. stramonium_ has frequently been employed in traditional medicine to treat a variety of ailments. It has also been used as a hallucinogen (of the [anticholinergic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticholinergic "Anticholinergic")/[antimuscarinic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimuscarinic "Antimuscarinic"), [deliriant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deliriant "Deliriant") type), taken [entheogenically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entheogen "Entheogen") to cause intense, sacred or occult visions.[[1]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-POWO-1)[[6]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-Schultes,_Richard_Evans_1979-6) It is unlikely to become a major drug of abuse owing to effects upon both mind and body frequently perceived as being highly [unpleasant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dysphoria "Dysphoria"), giving rise to a state of profound and long-lasting disorientation or [delirium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delirium "Delirium") ([anticholinergic syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxidrome#Anticholinergic "Toxidrome")) with a potentially fatal outcome. It contains [tropane alkaloids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropane_alkaloid "Tropane alkaloid") which are responsible for the psychoactive effects, and may be severely toxic.[[1]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-POWO-1)[[7]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-Glatstein-7)

_Datura stramonium_ is an erect, [annual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annual_plant "Annual plant"), freely branching herb that forms a bush 60 to 150 cm (2 to 5 ft) tall.[[8]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-Stace-8)[[9]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-Henkel-1911-p30-9)[[10]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-maud-desc-10)

The [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root "Root") is long, thick, fibrous, and white. The [stem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_stem "Plant stem") is stout, erect, leafy, smooth, and pale yellow-green to reddish purple in color. The stem forks off repeatedly into branches and each fork forms a leaf and a single, erect flower.[[10]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-maud-desc-10)

The leaves are about 8 to 20 cm (3–8 in) long, smooth, toothed,[[9]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-Henkel-1911-p30-9) soft, and irregularly undulated.[[10]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-maud-desc-10) The upper surface of the leaves is a darker green, and the bottom is a light green.[[9]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-Henkel-1911-p30-9) The leaves have a bitter and nauseating taste, which is imparted to extracts of the herb, and remains even after the leaves have been dried.[[10]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-maud-desc-10)

_Datura stramonium_ generally flowers throughout the summer. The fragrant flowers have a pleasing odour; are trumpet-shaped, white to creamy or violet, and 6 to 9 cm (2+1⁄2–3+1⁄2 in) long; and grow on short stems from either the [axils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axils "Axils") of the leaves or the places where the branches fork. The [calyx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calyx_\(botany\) "Calyx (botany)") is long and tubular, swollen at the bottom, and sharply angled, surmounted by five sharp teeth. The [corolla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corolla_\(flower\) "Corolla (flower)"), which is folded and only partially open, is white, funnel-shaped, and has prominent ribs. The flowers open at night, emitting a pleasant fragrance, and are fed upon by nocturnal moths.[[10]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-maud-desc-10)

The egg-shaped seed [capsule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capsule_\(botany\) "Capsule (botany)") is 3 to 8 cm (1–3 in) in diameter and either covered with spines or bald. At maturity, it splits into four chambers, each with dozens of small, black seeds.[[10]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/55854-Datura-stramonium#cite_note-maud-desc-10)

## White goosefoot
https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58127-Chenopodium-album
  
_**Chenopodium album**_ is a fast-growing [annual plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annual_plant "Annual plant") in the [flowering plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowering_plant "Flowering plant") family [Amaranthaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amaranthaceae "Amaranthaceae"). Though cultivated in some regions, the plant is elsewhere considered a [weed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weed "Weed"). Common names include **lamb's quarters**, **melde**, **goosefoot**, **wild spinach** and **fat-hen**, though several are also applied to other species of the genus _[Chenopodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chenopodium "Chenopodium")_, for which reason it is often distinguished as **white goosefoot**.[[3]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58127-Chenopodium-album#cite_note-bsbi-3)[[4]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58127-Chenopodium-album#cite_note-pier-4)[[5]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58127-Chenopodium-album#cite_note-fna-5)

It tends to grow upright at first, reaching heights of 10–150 centimetres (4–59 in), rarely to 3 m; it then typically becomes recumbent after flowering (due to the weight of the foliage and seeds) unless supported by other plants. The [leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaves "Leaves") are alternate and varied in appearance. The first leaves, near the base of the plant, are toothed and roughly diamond-shaped, 3–7 cm long and 3–6 cm broad. The leaves on the upper part of the flowering stems are entire and lanceolate-rhomboid, 1–5 cm long and 0.4–2 cm broad; they are waxy-coated, unwettable and mealy in appearance, with a whitish coat on the underside. The small [flowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower "Flower") are radially symmetrical and grow in small cymes on a dense branched [inflorescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflorescence "Inflorescence") 10–40 cm long.[[4]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58127-Chenopodium-album#cite_note-pier-4)[[5]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58127-Chenopodium-album#cite_note-fna-5)[[6]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58127-Chenopodium-album#cite_note-foc-6) Further, the flowers are bisexual and female, with five tepals which are mealy on outer surface, and shortly united at the base.[[7]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58127-Chenopodium-album#cite_note-vicflora-7) There are five stamens.[[7]](https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/58127-Chenopodium-album#cite_note-vicflora-7)
